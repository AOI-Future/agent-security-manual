# Verification and Testing

## Turning SHALL into pass or fail

A requirement you cannot test is an opinion with a serial number. Chapter 9 was written so that every SHALL is verifiable; this chapter is where the verification actually lives. Its job is to take each requirement and attach at least one test that returns an unambiguous pass or fail, so that "we conform" stops being a statement of intent and becomes a result you can show someone who does not trust you. This is the last link in the chain the book has enforced since Chapter 1 — Threat, Control, Requirement, Verification — and it is the link that makes the other three honest.

The plan is built to survive contact with an adversary who is specifically trying to make evaluation lie (TH-09). That means tests that run against production-representative conditions, tests that are repeatable rather than one-shot, and a record of results that cannot itself be tampered into a false pass. The chapter describes four verification methods, maps requirements to tests, states the operational rules, and then connects to the companion kit that executes the whole thing and produces a signed artifact.

## Four verification methods

Requirements fail in different ways, so they need different kinds of test. The plan uses four methods, each with a two-letter code that prefixes its test identifiers.

**VT-S — Static and structural verification.** Inspect configuration, policy, inventory, and code without running an attack: is the allowlist present, are tool definitions hashed, is the posture state machine wired, is the identity distinct from a human's. VT-S answers "is the control built and configured as required." It is cheap, deterministic, and the right method for requirements that are properties of the system's structure.

**VT-D — Dynamic adversarial verification.** Run actual attacks and observe behavior: fire prompt injections through retrieved content, attempt tool abuse, try to escalate privilege through delegation, attempt exfiltration. VT-D answers "does the control hold when someone pushes on it." It is the method for the injection and tool-abuse families, and it is where the red-team tooling is used.

**VT-E — Evidence and log verification.** Inspect the record the system produces: can the episode be reconstructed from logs, are secrets redacted, is the correlation ID threaded, is every runtime action bound to a policy version. VT-E answers "did the system record what it was supposed to, in a form an investigator can use" — and, separately, "is what it recorded true." Those are two questions, not one. A record can be complete on its face and still be an invention of the layer that wrote it, so a VT-E test is only finished when each item of evidence has been resolved back to a raw artifact the reporting layer does not author — a log line, a run identifier, a configuration hash — and the claim re-derived from that artifact rather than read off the summary. An assertion that cannot be resolved that way fails; it does not count as covered. VT-E verifies the audit layer that all incident response depends on, which is precisely why it cannot take that layer's word for anything.

**VT-A — Attestation and audit verification.** Confirm the higher-order properties: that the runtime posture actually transitioned on a trigger within its SLA, that reconciliation happened, that governance records exist, that the verification suite itself ran and was retained. VT-A answers "does the assurance process itself function as designed." It is the method for the runtime, governance, and meta-requirements.

The tool baseline supports these methods rather than defining them. A read-only posture scanner (**Hardshell v2**) performs the static VT-S sweep without itself holding write capability, which matters because a verification tool with production write access is a new attack surface. Adversarial testing draws on **PyRIT** (Microsoft's risk-identification toolkit) and **garak** (NVIDIA's LLM vulnerability scanner, v0.15.1) for the VT-D attack library. The tools are instruments; the methods are the contract.

## Requirement-to-test mapping

Every requirement maps to one or more tests, and the test's prefix tells you how it is checked. The table below is a *representative* slice of that mapping — enough rows to make the pattern legible, not the exhaustive matrix. The complete requirement-to-test master matrix, every SHALL bound to its full set of tests, is a living artifact: it grows each time a regression case is added, which is why it lives in the companion kit rather than being frozen onto a printed page. The kit both reports against these stable identifiers and enforces, through the coverage check (REQ-054), that no SHALL is ever left without a test.

Read the pattern rather than memorizing the rows. Structural requirements (allowlists, labeling presence, TTL config) get VT-S. Behavioral requirements (does labeling actually stop an injection, does validation actually reject a bad argument) get VT-D, often several, because one attack proves little and a family of attacks proves the control generalizes. Record-integrity requirements get VT-E. And the process-level requirements — posture transitions, delegation narrowing, approval metrics, and coverage itself — get VT-A, because what they assert is that a *process* ran correctly, which you confirm by attestation over its outputs. The injection family carries the most dynamic tests (VT-D-020 through VT-D-024) precisely because it is the hardest to close and the easiest to regress. Rows not shown here — memory reversibility, rollback exercises, the runtime-posture attestations — follow the same rule: each is a dynamic, evidence, or attestation test in the kit's master matrix, and the coverage check refuses to pass if any SHALL among them has gone untested.

The rows for REQ-055 through REQ-061 are a different shape of test, and they are worth reading closely, because they are the ones that fail when the trail is present but false. **VT-D-055** attempts, from the agent's own identity and from each of its tools, to alter or delete a record already written; the test passes only when every attempt is refused by the store rather than by the agent's own restraint, and **VT-E-055** reconciles the primary record against its out-of-band replica, treating any divergence as a failure regardless of which copy looks more plausible. **VT-D-056** approves one payload and substitutes another between approval and execution; a system that executes the substituted payload fails, and so does a system that executes the right payload but cannot show the digest binding the two. **VT-E-057** plants a known-false claim in the evidence the reporting layer consumes and requires the checker to go red — a negative control, mutation testing pointed at evidence rather than at code — while **VT-A-080** confirms that the canary required by REQ-057 was present in every retained run, so a green history cannot be a history of a harness that had quietly stopped being able to fail. In each case the assertion under test is not "the artifact exists" but "the artifact could not have been fabricated by the thing it describes."

Four of them ask a further question: not whether the artifact is true, but whether the party asserting it was entitled to. **VT-A-081** takes a sample of conformance verdicts and traces each back to the identity that issued it, failing whenever that identity belongs to the assessed system, its owner, or a component the assessed system controls; **VT-E-058** attempts the same thing actively, presenting the assessed component's own credentials to the mechanism that records conformance and failing if a verdict about itself can be written. **VT-E-059** attacks the denominator by supplying an empty, null, or errored reference set and by mutating the expected list, and passes only when the run refuses to report a score at all rather than reporting a full one against an empty list — the fail-closed half of the same test also confirms that a check whose input never arrived is counted as not-run instead of silently disappearing from the total; **VT-A-082** then verifies that the reference set actually used in a retained run is the externally held, versioned one, by comparing recorded version identifiers rather than by asking the reporting tool which list it consulted. **VT-A-083** samples the append-only evidence log for inclusion proofs and confirms that at least one monitor outside the reporting system has pulled and checked the log within the review period, since a log nobody independently reads detects nothing. **VT-A-084** samples supplier records and fails wherever the only evidence supporting an admitted supplier is a document the supplier wrote about itself with no accepted-risk entry to acknowledge that fact.

The last five rows ask the question underneath all of those: whether the thing being measured is the thing the requirement is about. **VT-A-090** reads each verification record and fails wherever a result is reported against a stand-in without naming both the stand-in and the property it stands for — a result recorded as "labeling present" with no statement of what presence was taken to establish. **VT-E-090** is the divergence test itself: it constructs a state in which the proxy holds and the property does not — a labeler that runs on every document and misclassifies most of them, an evaluation that completes with its assertions disabled, a flag that flips at a control plane no enforcement point consults — and fails whenever verification still reports a pass. Its expected result is held outside the system under test, for the same reason REQ-059's denominator is. **VT-A-091** samples reports and fails any that state findings without stating scope: checks not selected, not applicable, or not run, and the conditions the method used could not have reached. **VT-D-060** fires a production-safe signal designed to trigger the monitoring required by REQ-031 and fails if no alert results, while **VT-A-092** checks every monitored source for input older than its declared expected interval and raises that as a failure of the monitoring rather than recording a quiet period. **VT-D-025** runs the labeler and the tool risk classifier against a labeled reference set held outside them, reporting the error rate with trust-increasing misclassifications — untrusted content labeled trusted, a high-risk tool classified low — counted separately from the aggregate; **VT-E-091** confirms that the accepted accuracy level and the metric expressing it were declared and retained with the run, since a rate with no declared threshold is a number nobody has agreed to be held to. **VT-D-061** attempts a revoked action at the enforcement point after a kill switch or a posture transition and passes only when the attempt is refused and both the attempt and the refusal are recorded, and **VT-A-093** fails any posture change recorded as applied without such a confirmation. Together these are the tests that fail when the proxy passed and the thing it stood for did not.

The next two requirements ask what each check was entitled to conclude and whether each accepted lesson stayed learned. **VT-A-094** audits the declarations REQ-067 requires: it samples checks across the evaluation, monitoring, and verification suites and fails any check that does not declare both its claim and its oracle level, and any whose recorded claim exceeds what the declared oracle can discriminate — a "response correct" conclusion resting on a transport oracle that knows only that a 200 came back. **VT-D-062** is the divergence battery: four constructed cases in which a weaker oracle passes and the declared one fails — a 200 whose well-formed body carries the wrong answer, a redirect to a login page served under a 200, an HTML error page inside a JSON envelope, and a query whose filter was silently dropped so the response is right in form and wrong in scope — each detectable only by an oracle at the declared level, so a suite that passes the battery has demonstrated its oracles discriminate what its claims assert. The last case is named deliberately, because the canary of REQ-057 cannot catch it: a check that still fires but has silently lost part of its range is indistinguishable, to a liveness test, from one that never lost anything. **VT-A-095** samples the accepted findings recorded against this specification and fails any that lack both a corresponding verification test and a changelog entry referencing the finding, unless a reason the lesson does not generalize was recorded in their place — the test that fails if REQ-068's loop is ever quietly abandoned.

The final row asks whether a run traversed the set it claims to cover. **VT-E-092** obtains the externally held, versioned target set independently of the reporting layer and reconciles it one for one against terminal events emitted at the parsing or analysis boundary and retained by a collector the reporting layer cannot alter. It fails on a missing, extra, or duplicate target; a count mismatch; an unresolved terminal state; a target record with no run identifier or timestamp; or a synthetic `analyzed` record for a target whose parser or executor emitted no completion event. The fixture includes a legitimate zero-finding run, because zero is valid when the reconciliation is complete. **VT-D-063** interrupts a run after a proper subset of its targets by injecting a timeout, an unhandled exception, and a loss of execution telemetry in separate cases; it passes only when the remaining targets are accounted for as not-run and the run is reported as failed or incomplete rather than PASS or a complete zero-finding result. Findings from the completed subset may remain available under that incomplete status. Together the tests separate the truth of the findings from the completeness of the traversal that produced them.

## Operational rules

The tests are only trustworthy if they are run and recorded under discipline. Four rules govern that.

Results are written to a dated report at `reports/<date>_verification_report.md`, so every run is a distinct, retained artifact rather than a mutable dashboard. For each test the report records only the identifier and pass or fail; the run header additionally records the total-target, analyzed, processing-error, and not-run counts required by REQ-069 and a reference to the retained machine-readable execution record. Payloads, transcripts, captured data, and target paths stay out of the human-readable report, because a verification report that quotes the attack it ran becomes a manual for that attack and a place for a secret to leak. Any failure is written to `data/change_queue.jsonl`, which is the same queue the continuous-update and runtime-reconciliation processes consume, so a failed test automatically becomes tracked work rather than a line someone hopes to remember. And secrets are never recorded in any test output: where a test must handle a credential, it appears as `[REDACTED]` plus a locator, consistent with the rule that holds everywhere else in the book. The point of these rules is that the verification process is itself hardened against the threats it verifies — it does not leak, it does not lie by omission, and its failures do not evaporate.

## Regression, not a gate

The single most important operational property is that this is a standing suite, not a launch checklist. Chapter 7 argued that evaluation has to run continuously because every model, tool, RAG source, prompt, or permission change can re-open a closed case; the verification plan is the machinery that makes that argument executable. Every failure that has ever been fixed stays in the suite as a permanent regression case, so a fix cannot silently rot and a change cannot quietly re-open an old hole without a red test to show for it. **REQ-054** closes the loop on the plan itself: the coverage check (VT-A-070, VT-A-071) verifies that every SHALL still maps to at least one test, so the suite cannot be hollowed out by adding requirements without adding tests. A verification plan that is not itself verified is just another unchecked claim.

There is a second loop underneath that one, and it took a reader to point it out. The coverage check is executed by the same companion kit that writes the report — so the tool certifying that the plan is complete is the tool whose completeness is in question. Every assurance layer has this seam somewhere, and naming it is not a confession of a bug so much as an admission that self-verification has a floor it cannot get below. **REQ-058** sets that floor: the verdict has to be issued under an identity separate from the assessed system's, over artifacts the assessed system cannot write. In practice this means the coverage check should be re-run by the operator's own tooling against the raw artifacts, not merely read out of the kit's summary, and that the kit's report is a claim submitted for assessment rather than the assessment itself. The same standards bodies arrived here first — an assessor independent of the system assessed in NIST SP 800-53 CA-2(1), an auditor who does not audit their own work in ISO/IEC 42001 clause 9.2, a notified body barred from also consulting on compliance in Article 31 of the EU AI Act, and build provenance that the tenant it describes cannot forge in SLSA. What none of them can supply is the will to run the check; that part is yours.

Coverage, though, is still a presence check — it counts links, not truths. A suite can map every SHALL to a test and still be broken in the one way that matters, which is to have lost the ability to return red at all: a harness pointed at a stale fixture, an assertion that swallows its own exception, a checker reading a summary that the reporting layer wrote about itself. That is why **REQ-057** requires a known-failing control case on every run and why a run in which the canary passes is recorded as a failed run. Coverage answers "is every requirement tested"; the canary answers "is the testing still real." Both questions have to be asked, because an adversary working on TH-09 does not need to remove your tests. It is enough to leave them in place and make them green.

The canary is one instance of a more general problem, and this revision names the general case. Nearly every layer of assurance in this book measures a stand-in for the thing it cares about. Coverage stands in for testedness. A completed run stands in for a working control. A silent monitor stands in for an absence of events. A recorded posture transition stands in for a capability that is actually gone. The presence of a labeler stands in for labels that are correct. Substitution is not a defect — a property you cannot observe directly has to be approached through one you can — but every substitution can come apart, and when it does the record stays green while the property it described is gone. **REQ-062** through **REQ-066** close that gap: name the stand-in and the property together in the record, keep a test that fails when the two diverge, say in every report what the run could not have seen, exercise the monitoring rather than trusting its silence, measure the accuracy of the labels the rest of the chain depends on, and make a revoked capability demonstrate that it is unusable. The ladder this book has been climbing since Chapter 1 has seven rungs — is the control there, does it hold, is the record true, is the judge entitled, is the measurement measuring what it claims to, does the claim stay within what its oracle can discriminate, and did the run finish over the target set it claims to cover. The sixth rung is where **REQ-067** stands; **REQ-069** adds the seventh, because even a sound oracle says nothing about targets it never reached. **REQ-068** keeps the ladder growing by binding every accepted finding to a test and a changelog line, so a rung once climbed cannot be quietly descended. No rung substitutes for the ones below it, and the top rungs are the ones an adversary working on TH-09 reaches for last, because they are the ones that leave everything looking correct.

## The companion kit and the signed artifact

Everything in this chapter can be done by hand, and the book tells you exactly what to check so that you can. What the book does not do — because a book cannot — is execute the suite and hand you evidence. That is the companion kit, sold separately, and this is the seam where the free explanation meets the paid tooling.

The kit runs the full method set: the Hardshell v2 static sweep, the PyRIT and garak adversarial battery, the log and evidence inspection, and the attestation checks. It applies the operational rules automatically — dated reports, failures routed to the change queue, secrets redacted — and it consolidates the whole run into a **single artifact: one JSON payload carrying every test identifier and its pass or fail, rendered to a PDF a CISO can read, and sealed with an RFC 3161 trusted timestamp.** The timestamp is the part that matters to someone who does not trust you: it proves the results existed at a specific time and have not been edited since, which turns "we tested this" from an assertion into a verifiable record.

Be precise about what that seal does and does not establish, because the distinction is the whole subject of this chapter. A trusted timestamp is tamper-evidence *after* the fact. It proves the artifact has not changed since it was sealed; it proves nothing about whether the artifact was true when it was written. A fabricated result seals exactly as cleanly as an honest one, and a report that invents its own evidence will carry a perfectly valid RFC 3161 token. The signature is therefore the last link, not the first: what makes the sealed artifact worth anything is that every claim inside it was resolved back to a raw artifact the reporting layer did not author (VT-E), that the record it draws on could not be rewritten by the system it describes (REQ-055, VT-D-055), and that the harness which produced the greens was demonstrably still able to produce a red (REQ-057, VT-E-057). Seal that, and the timestamp means something. Seal an unverified summary, and you have proved only the date on which you committed to it.

That single signed artifact is the concrete answer to Chapter 1's last question — *can we prove all of the above to someone who does not trust us* — and it is the deliverable an enterprise buyer or a regulator actually asks to see.

Verification is where the book's chain terminates and where its claims become checkable. What remains is the human accountability that surrounds the technical system — who owns the risk, who reviewed the decision, who is answerable when a control fails. That is governance, and it is the subject of the final chapter.
